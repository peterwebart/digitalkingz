SEO vs GEO vs AEO: The Future of Search
Introduction
Search has changed from a list of links into a multi-surface discovery system. A prospective
buyer may still search Google for “enterprise CRM software,” but they may also ask Google AI
Overviews, ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini, Copilot, or an internal enterprise assistant: “Which
CRM supports Salesforce migration, SSO, EU data residency, and a 90-day implementation
timeline?”
That shift has created three overlapping disciplines: search engine optimization (SEO), answer
engine optimization (AEO), and generative engine optimization (GEO). Each addresses a
different part of the modern search journey:
SEO helps relevant pages get discovered, crawled, indexed, ranked, and clicked.
AEO helps systems extract a concise, accurate answer to a specific question.
GEO focuses on whether a brand, product, or source is credible and useful enough to be
represented in generative AI answers.
For engineers, buyers, procurement managers, and technical decision makers, the important
point is not which acronym wins. It is whether a company’s information is technically
accessible, factually reliable, easy to understand, differentiated from commodity content, and
persuasive enough to influence a purchase decision.
This guide explains SEO vs GEO vs AEO, where the concepts overlap, which initiatives deserve
priority, and how to build a search strategy that supports both conventional search results and
AI-mediated buyer research. Google’s current guidance is clear that established SEO
fundamentals still apply to AI features in Search: allow crawling, make important text
discoverable through internal links, provide a good page experience, and publish helpful,
reliable, people-first content. [1]
Executive answer: SEO creates the technical and content foundation for discoverability.
AEO makes important information easier to extract as direct answers. GEO extends that
work by strengthening the evidence, entity clarity, expertise, and source quality that
can make a brand more likely to be accurately represented in generative search
experiences.

What is SEO and what business problem does it solve?
SEO is the process of improving a website’s ability to appear for relevant searches. In practical
B2B terms, it helps potential customers find product pages, technical documentation,
integrations, comparisons, use cases, support resources, compliance materials, and research
content when they are actively evaluating a problem or vendor.
SEO is not simply “ranking for keywords.” Effective SEO combines technical accessibility,
information architecture, content quality, search-intent alignment, credibility, and conversion
design.
For a technical company, the business problem is often straightforward: buyers have questions
before they speak to sales, but the company’s best answers are hard to find, poorly structured,
gated, outdated, or spread across product documentation, support articles, case studies, and
internal knowledge bases.
A strong SEO program turns that fragmented information into an accessible commercial asset.

Which SEO foundations matter most?
A durable SEO strategy normally includes the following components.

Crawlability and indexability
Search engines need to access important pages. A company can produce excellent content, but
it will not help search visibility if crawlers are blocked by robots rules, authentication
requirements, poor JavaScript rendering, incorrect canonical tags, noindex directives, or
broken internal navigation.
Key checks include:
Important product, documentation, and commercial pages return a valid 200 status code.
Robots.txt does not unintentionally block search crawlers.
Pages intended to rank do not contain accidental noindex tags.
Canonical URLs are logical and consistent.
JavaScript-rendered content is available to crawlers.
XML sitemaps contain indexable canonical URLs.
Pagination, faceted navigation, and filters do not create large duplicate-content problems.
Important assets are reachable through crawlable internal links.

Information architecture
Information architecture determines whether both users and crawlers can understand the
relationship between pages.

Search-intent alignment
Search intent is the underlying goal behind a query. A person searching “what is SOC 2”
probably wants a clear explanation. A person searching “SOC 2 compliant project management
software” is closer to vendor evaluation. A person searching “[brand] SOC 2 report” likely wants
proof.
Search intent should shape the page format, depth, proof points, and call to action.

Content quality and usefulness
Google’s people-first content guidance encourages site owners to consider who created content,
how it was produced, and why it exists. It also emphasizes first-hand expertise, a clear site
focus, and whether readers leave having achieved their goal. [2]
That matters because technical buyers can detect generic content quickly. They want specifics:

Conversion design
Traffic without commercial outcomes has limited value. SEO pages should guide the right
audience toward a relevant next step without disrupting the informational experience.
Examples:
A technical comparison page can link to a migration assessment.
A compliance guide can offer a security documentation package.
A product-integration page can offer a technical consultation.
A benchmark report can invite readers to request a custom ROI analysis.

Which SEO metrics matter to a B2B buying team?
Avoid measuring SEO solely through rankings or raw sessions. Those metrics can be useful
diagnostics, but they are not the business outcome.
Track a balanced set of metrics:
Non-branded organic impressions for strategic categories.
Organic clicks from high-intent commercial queries.
Rankings for category, solution, comparison, integration, and alternative queries.
Organic conversions, including demos, trials, consultations, and resource downloads.
Conversion rate by landing page and query intent.
Organic-assisted pipeline and influenced revenue.
Engagement with product documentation and integration pages.
Crawl and indexing coverage for important commercial pages.
Share of search against primary competitors.
Content refresh rate for time-sensitive, technical, or regulated topics.
Practical example: A cybersecurity vendor may see modest traffic on a page titled
“What Is Zero Trust?” but significant pipeline from pages such as “Zero Trust Network
Access Requirements,” “ZTNA vs VPN for Enterprise,” and “How to Implement Zero
Trust With Microsoft Entra ID.” The latter pages align more closely with technical and
procurement-stage intent.

What is AEO and how does it help content become a direct answer?
Answer engine optimization, or AEO, is the practice of making content easier for systems to
interpret, extract, and present as an answer to a user’s question.
AEO is not about writing robotic answers for machines. It is about communicating clearly
enough that a human reader, search engine, AI assistant, voice interface, or internal knowledge
system can identify the central answer quickly.
AEO is especially relevant for:
Direct-answer search results
Featured snippets
“People Also Ask” questions
Voice-search queries
AI Overviews and AI summaries
Help-center search
Product-support experiences
RAG-based enterprise assistants

Chatbot responses based on company documentation

How should teams structure content for answer extraction?
The best AEO content answers a question immediately, then provides context, caveats, proof,
and next steps.
A useful framework is:
1. State the direct answer in the first one to three sentences.
2. Define the terms used in the answer.
3. Explain when the answer applies.
4. Explain exceptions, tradeoffs, or limitations.
5. Provide evidence, examples, or implementation details.
6. Link to the next relevant decision point.
For example:
Question: Does schema markup improve AI-search visibility?
Direct answer: Schema markup can help search systems understand structured
information on a page, but it does not guarantee rankings, rich results, AI Overview
inclusion, or citations. It works best when it accurately reflects useful, visible content
and supports an already accessible, well-structured page.
That is better than beginning with a long generic history of schema.

What makes an answer useful to both people and machines?
A high-quality answer generally has five characteristics.

It answers the actual question
Avoid burying the answer under background context. If the heading asks “What is GEO?”, the
first paragraph should define GEO.

It uses precise language
Technical content should avoid vague phrases such as “seamless,” “revolutionary,” “best-inclass,” or “future-proof” unless the page explains what those claims mean.
Instead of writing:
Our software delivers seamless enterprise-grade security.
Write:
The platform supports SAML 2.0 single sign-on, role-based access controls, audit logs,
and encryption in transit and at rest. Availability of individual controls depends on the
selected plan and deployment model.

It includes scope and constraints
AEO does not mean oversimplification. A short answer can include a limitation.
For example:
Kubernetes can improve portability and scaling for containerized workloads, but it
adds operational complexity and may be unnecessary for a small application with
predictable traffic.

It is supported by visible evidence
Do not hide crucial proof in vague marketing claims. Add links to documentation, test methods,
specifications, customer evidence, certifications, or source material where appropriate.

It has a clear page hierarchy
Use headings that explain the user’s next question rather than headings such as “Overview,”
“Additional Information,” or “More Details.”
Better headings include:
What is the difference between AEO and GEO?
Which pages should be optimized for AI search first?
Does FAQ schema guarantee rich results?
How do procurement teams measure AI-search visibility?

Does FAQ schema guarantee AI or rich-result visibility?
No. FAQ schema does not guarantee rich results, direct-answer placement, AI Overview
visibility, or inclusion in any external AI system.
Structured data helps machines understand page information and may make a page eligible for
certain enhanced search appearances when it meets the relevant requirements. Google
recommends JSON-LD as a supported format and requires structured data to be accurate,
current, and representative of the visible page content. [3]
For most commercial websites, FAQ schema should not be treated as a shortcut. Google
currently limits FAQ rich results primarily to well-known, authoritative government and
health-focused sites. [4]
That does not mean an FAQ section is useless. A visible, well-written FAQ can still:
Help users find answers faster.
Improve page clarity.
Support internal-linking opportunities.
Assist support and sales enablement.
Provide concise answers that may be useful to search and AI systems.
Reduce repetitive presales questions.
The correct approach is: write the FAQ because it helps the reader, then add markup only if it
accurately represents the visible content and is appropriate for the page.

Common AEO mistakes
Writing a 40-word definition without explanation, evidence, or context.
Creating hundreds of shallow FAQ pages aimed only at capturing long-tail keywords.
Marking up content that users cannot see.
Using FAQPage markup for content that is actually a discussion forum or multi-answer
community page.
Adding schema that is unrelated to the page’s primary purpose.
Repeating the same keyword unnaturally in headings and answers.
Assuming that every question deserves its own page.
Replacing technical documentation with generic marketing copy.
Publishing unsupported claims simply because competitors make them.
Expert recommendation: Build answer-first sections into important commercial and
technical pages rather than creating isolated FAQ content at scale. A detailed integration
page, for example, should answer setup, authentication, data flow, limitations,
permissions, troubleshooting, and support ownership in the context where buyers need
that information.

What is GEO and how is it different from AEO?
Generative engine optimization, or GEO, is an emerging term for improving how a company,
product, expertise, and content are understood and represented in generative AI experiences.
GEO is closely related to AEO, but it is broader. AEO concentrates on making an individual
answer easy to extract. GEO considers whether the entire source, brand, and entity are credible
enough to be used in a generated response about a topic, comparison, recommendation, or
technical decision.
The terminology is still evolving. Different platforms, consultants, and technology providers
may use GEO, AEO, AI SEO, LLM optimization, or generative search optimization in overlapping
ways. The important operational distinction is not the label; it is whether the work improves
genuine information quality and buyer trust.

What signals can make a brand more reference-worthy in AI search?
No public platform provides a complete, stable list of “GEO ranking factors.” Teams should be
skeptical of anyone who claims to know a universal formula for being cited by all AI tools.
However, the following quality signals make content more useful and defensible across search
systems.

Original evidence
AI systems and human researchers both benefit from sources that contain information
unavailable elsewhere.
Examples include:
Original survey data with methodology.
Product benchmarks and reproducible testing criteria.
Anonymized customer-performance data.
Engineering postmortems.
Industry datasets.
Regulatory analysis from qualified experts.
Tested migration frameworks.
Detailed implementation playbooks.
Public APIs, changelogs, and accurate technical documentation.
A generic article that restates existing advice is easier to replace than a primary source
containing unique data or direct experience.

Subject-matter expertise
A technical guide should show who wrote it and why they are qualified. A security
implementation guide authored or reviewed by a security engineer is more credible than an
anonymous marketing post.
Useful trust elements include:
Named author and reviewer.
Role, experience, certifications, or relevant background.
Links to professional profiles or prior work where appropriate.
Editorial-review date.
Clear ownership of technical claims.
A mechanism for corrections or feedback.

Entity clarity
An entity is a recognizable organization, person, product, location, or concept. For B2B
companies, entity clarity means being consistent about what the company is, what it sells, who
it serves, how its products relate to each other, and what claims it can substantiate.
Maintain consistent information across:
Organization pages.
Product pages.

Documentation.
Press releases.
Business profiles.
Social profiles.
Partner directories.
Analyst profiles.
Industry associations.
Reputable third-party publications.

Factual consistency and freshness
Contradictory information creates risk. A pricing page, help-center article, sales deck, schema
markup, marketplace listing, and AI assistant should not describe the product differently.
Review content when:
A feature is launched, deprecated, or renamed.
A certification expires or is renewed.
Pricing changes.
Integration functionality changes.
A regulation changes.
Company ownership, leadership, or contact information changes.
A benchmark becomes outdated.

Third-party validation
An organization’s own site is important, but external references can add credibility. Relevant
validation may include customer reviews, partner listings, reputable editorial coverage, analyst
reports, open-source contributions, conference presentations, academic references, and
industry awards.
The goal is not to buy low-quality backlinks or mass-produce mentions. The goal is to earn
credible references by contributing useful information.

Why is E-E-A-T critical for GEO?
E-E-A-T stands for experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness. It is a useful
content-quality framework because it shifts attention away from keyword manipulation and
toward evidence.
Google explains that its systems look for a mix of factors that can help identify content
demonstrating experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trustworthiness. [2]

For GEO, E-E-A-T matters because generated answers create a higher risk of oversimplification,
outdated information, and unsupported claims. A source that documents its methodology,
authorship, limitations, and evidence is more valuable than a source that uses persuasive
language without proof.

How can a company demonstrate experience?
Experience is particularly important for technical and high-consideration content.
Use:
Real implementation examples.
Screenshots or diagrams where permitted.
Field-tested workflows.
Lessons from migrations, incidents, or deployments.
Customer stories with measurable outcomes.
Product-specific technical explanations.
Tradeoffs and known limitations.
Step-by-step configurations tested by the team.
Quotes from qualified engineers or practitioners.
Avoid fabricating experience. If an article is based on desk research rather than direct testing,
say so.
Callout: A credible limitation improves trust.
“Our migration approach is designed for teams moving from legacy on-premises
systems to our cloud platform. It may not be suitable for organizations that require fully
air-gapped deployment.”
This statement is more credible than claiming universal suitability.

Common GEO mistakes
Calling basic keyword optimization “GEO” without improving source quality.
Creating AI-generated articles that repeat the same information found on competitor sites.
Making claims of guaranteed AI citations.
Publishing unverified comparison tables.
Hiding authorship, date, methodology, or commercial relationships.
Treating every external mention as equally valuable.
Building pages around tools or platforms without checking whether the information is
current.
Ignoring technical accessibility because the work is labeled “AI search.”
Attempting to manipulate crawlers with misleading schema, invisible text, or fake reviews.
Monitoring AI mentions without measuring business impact or answer accuracy.

Is GEO replacing SEO?
No. GEO should extend SEO, not replace it.
The strongest GEO program starts with the same fundamentals that support conventional
search: crawlable pages, accessible content, useful internal links, technical quality, accurate
structured data, page experience, clear information architecture, and helpful people-first
content.
Google’s guidance for AI features explicitly says that the same foundational SEO best practices
remain applicable. It highlights crawl access, internal linking, textual accessibility, highquality visual support where useful, matching structured data to visible content, and strong
page experience. [1]

Why does SEO remain foundational?
AI systems still need accessible source material
If critical product information exists only in a login-protected dashboard, an unindexed PDF,
an image, a video without a transcript, or a poorly rendered JavaScript component, it is less
discoverable and less useful.

Buyers still need source pages
AI summaries may answer basic questions, but technical buyers often need to verify the
source. They want specifications, security details, integration requirements, pricing,
documentation, support policies, and evidence that the vendor understands their context.

Complex purchases need depth
A generated response might say that a platform supports SSO. A procurement team will still
need to know:
Which identity providers are supported?
Is SAML or OIDC available?
Which plans include SSO?
Can SCIM provisioning be configured?
Are audit logs exportable?
What is the SLA?
Is the feature available in all regions?
What implementation effort is required?
These details belong on a company’s owned website.

SEO supports measurement and conversion
SEO can be connected to analytics, CRM outcomes, assisted conversions, pipeline, and revenue.
AI mentions may be meaningful, but they should be measured alongside—not instead of—
business outcomes.

Which strategy should a company prioritize first?
The correct priority depends on the company’s current maturity, commercial goals, and
technical constraints.

Prioritize SEO first when the foundation is weak
Start with SEO if any of the following are true:
Important commercial pages are not indexed.
Product documentation is hard to discover.
The site has serious crawl, rendering, performance, or duplication problems.
Important content is not linked internally.
Product, pricing, security, and integration information is fragmented.
The company has little visibility for high-intent category searches.
Content does not map to the buyer journey.
Existing pages lack clear conversion paths.
Technical claims are outdated or inconsistent.
AEO and GEO cannot reliably compensate for inaccessible or untrustworthy source material.

Prioritize AEO when buyer questions are clear and repetitive
AEO should be a priority when sales, support, solutions engineering, and procurement teams
repeatedly answer the same questions.
Common examples:
Does the platform integrate with Microsoft Teams?
What data is collected?
Is the product GDPR compliant?
How long does implementation take?
Does the API support webhooks?
Can users export audit logs?
What is the difference between the Professional and Enterprise plans?
How does the product compare with a known competitor?
Build pages and answer blocks around these questions. Start with high-value questions that
affect conversion, qualification, security review, or implementation.

Invest in GEO when authority and representation are strategic
GEO is particularly valuable when:
Buyers use AI tools to research the category.
The category has high stakes, technical complexity, or long sales cycles.
Your company has genuine expertise or original data.
Brand accuracy matters because misinformation could affect pipeline.
Competitors are frequently mentioned in industry discussions.
You need to improve third-party validation and category authority.
Your content is useful but too generic to be distinctive.

Step 10: Maintain a controlled prompt set
Do not rely on random searches. Build a repeatable library of prompts such as:

Record results by date, platform, location if relevant, brand inclusion, accuracy, cited sources,
and competitor mentions.

deterministic rank tracker.

What should procurement teams ask an SEO, AEO, or GEO provider?
A credible provider should discuss business outcomes, technical quality, content governance,
and measurement—not only keywords, backlinks, or AI citations.
Ask these questions during vendor evaluation:
1. How will you connect visibility improvements to qualified pipeline, conversion quality,
and revenue?
2. Which technical SEO issues will you audit, prioritize, and help resolve?
3. How will you identify commercially important buyer questions?
4. Which content formats will you recommend for product, integration, comparison, and
compliance topics?
5. How will you involve internal engineers, product leaders, and subject-matter experts?
6. What is your approach to E-E-A-T, editorial review, author attribution, and factual
accuracy?
7. How will you distinguish evidence-based GEO work from unsupported AI-citation

8. Which platforms and prompt sets will you monitor?
9. How will you measure brand accuracy and competitor representation in AI answers?

Warning signs when evaluating a provider
Be cautious when a vendor:
Guarantees placement in AI answers.
Promises a fixed number of citations without explaining the methodology.
Recommends publishing high volumes of unreviewed AI-generated pages.
Cannot explain technical SEO or content governance.
Does not ask about your audience, product, sales cycle, or conversion goals.
Focuses only on traffic while ignoring qualified demand.
Suggests misleading schema, hidden text, fake reviews, or artificial citations.
Cannot explain who will verify technical claims.

Industry best practices for SEO, AEO, and GEO
Build for a real audience first
Content should be useful even if search engines and AI assistants did not exist. Google’s peoplefirst guidance asks whether there is an intended audience, whether the content demonstrates
first-hand expertise, and whether users leave having achieved their goal. [2]

Make important content available as text
A product screenshot, video, or PDF can be valuable, but it should not be the only place where
critical information exists. Provide descriptive text, transcripts, captions, and HTML-based
documentation where possible.

Use structured data accurately
Structured data should reflect the visible page content. Google advises using the most specific
appropriate type and warns against misleading markup, fake reviews, and irrelevant structured
data. [3]
Use schema as a semantic enhancement, not a ranking shortcut.

Publish content that has a reason to exist
Before publishing, ask:
What question does this answer?
Who is the audience?
What evidence do we have?
What does this add that existing pages do not?
Who will maintain it?
What action should a qualified reader take next?

Treat documentation as a growth asset
Documentation is often one of the most valuable SEO, AEO, and GEO resources a technical
company has. It answers implementation questions, demonstrates product maturity, supports
engineers, reduces sales friction, and provides detailed evidence that generic content cannot
match.

Build internal links around decisions
Link pages based on the reader’s next decision:
A category guide links to a buyer’s checklist.
A solution page links to product capabilities.
A product page links to integrations and security controls.
An integration page links to setup documentation.
A comparison page links to migration resources.
A case study links to an implementation guide.

Refresh critical content
Review content more frequently when it includes:
Product details.
Pricing.
Security and compliance claims.
Regulation.
Technical specifications.
Benchmarks.
Integrations.
Competitive comparisons.
Industry statistics.

Common mistakes to avoid
Mistake: Treating SEO, AEO, and GEO as separate silos
The result is usually duplicated content, fragmented reporting, and inconsistent claims. Create
one visibility strategy with separate workstreams rather than three disconnected programs.

Mistake: Publishing generic AI-written content at scale
Low-effort, interchangeable content may create pages but rarely creates authority. Prioritize
expert input, original research, real examples, and a clear point of view.

Mistake: Optimizing only for top-of-funnel traffic
High traffic does not necessarily mean high commercial value. Balance educational content
with comparison, integration, security, implementation, pricing, and alternative pages.

Mistake: Hiding important details
Do not require a demo to find basic technical requirements, supported integrations,
compliance status, or product limitations. Gated content has a place, but hiding every answer
creates buyer friction.

Mistake: Using vague marketing language
Replace broad claims with evidence. Instead of “fast implementation,” provide a typical
timeline, dependencies, scope, and a case example.

Mistake: Forgetting content maintenance
An outdated technical page can harm trust more than no page at all. Assign ownership and
review schedules.

Mistake: Measuring vanity metrics
A rise in impressions, traffic, or AI mentions is not enough. Track qualified engagement,
conversion quality, pipeline, revenue influence, and answer accuracy.

Expert recommendations
1. Fix technical SEO before pursuing advanced GEO tactics. A crawlable, indexable, welllinked website is the baseline for every search surface.
2. Prioritize commercial buyer questions. Start with the questions that influence evaluation,
implementation, security review, procurement, and conversion.
3. Use engineers and product experts as source material. Marketing should turn expertise
into understandable content, not replace expertise with generic copy.
4. Create original evidence. Research, benchmarks, methodologies, case studies, and tested
implementation guidance are more durable than rewritten summaries.
5. Write direct answers, then add depth. This improves usability without sacrificing
technical accuracy.
6. Make limitations visible. Transparent constraints improve trust and help qualify leads.
7. Use schema carefully. Validate markup, ensure it matches visible content, and do not
expect it to guarantee rich results or AI visibility. Google recommends testing structured
data with its Rich Results Test and schema validation tools. [5]
8. Build a repeatable AI-search monitoring program. Measure brand inclusion and accuracy
across a stable set of real buyer prompts.
9. Connect reporting to commercial outcomes. Track how search visibility contributes to
lead quality, pipeline, revenue, and sales efficiency.
10. Treat GEO as reputation work as well as content work. Your website, technical
documentation, expert authors, customer evidence, external references, and factual
consistency all shape how a brand can be understood.

Summary
SEO, AEO, and GEO are not competing strategies. They are three connected ways of improving
how a company is discovered, understood, trusted, and selected.
SEO ensures that important content can be found through search.
AEO ensures that key buyer questions receive concise, accurate, and extractable answers.
GEO focuses on creating the credibility, evidence, entity clarity, and differentiated
expertise that support accurate representation in generative search experiences.
For technical and B2B organizations, the winning strategy is not to chase every AI trend. It is to
build an accessible site, publish useful and evidence-led content, answer real buyer questions,
maintain factual accuracy, demonstrate expertise, and measure commercial impact.
The future of search will include more generated answers and fewer simple click paths for
some queries. But that makes owned source content more important, not less. When buyers
need verification, technical depth, comparison criteria, implementation requirements, and
trustworthy evidence, they still need strong source pages.

FAQ
What is the difference between SEO, AEO, and GEO?
SEO improves overall organic discoverability. AEO makes individual answers easier for search
and answer systems to extract. GEO focuses on strengthening a brand’s likelihood of being
accurately understood, included, or referenced in generative AI responses.

Is GEO the same as AI SEO?
Not exactly. AI SEO is a broad, informal term. GEO usually refers more specifically to visibility
and representation in generative search experiences, while SEO includes technical
accessibility, content relevance, site structure, and traditional organic search performance.

Is GEO replacing SEO?
No. GEO extends SEO. SEO fundamentals—crawlability, internal linking, page experience,
useful textual content, and accurate structured data—remain relevant for AI-search features. [1]

Does AEO require schema markup?
No. AEO starts with clear writing, direct answers, logical headings, and useful page structure.
Schema can help systems interpret content, but it should accurately match visible page content
and does not guarantee enhanced results or AI visibility. [3]

Can a company guarantee AI citations?
No. No credible provider can guarantee citations or inclusion in ChatGPT, Perplexity, Google AI
Overviews, Gemini, Copilot, or another AI system. Outputs can vary by query, source
availability, system behavior, location, date, and context.

What content supports GEO best?
Content supported by genuine expertise and original evidence is most durable. Examples
include original research, technical documentation, benchmarks, customer case studies,
expert analysis, implementation guides, methodologies, and accurate product data.

How can a B2B company measure GEO?
Use a controlled library of high-value buyer prompts. Track brand inclusion, accuracy, cited
sources where visible, competitor mentions, AI referral traffic, branded search trends,
conversions, and influenced pipeline.

Should engineers participate in SEO and GEO?
Yes. Engineers help ensure technical accuracy, documentation depth, implementation
examples, performance improvements, and evidence-based content. Their input is especially
valuable for integration, architecture, security, developer, and compliance topics.

Is FAQ schema useful for commercial websites?
A visible FAQ section can be useful for users, but FAQ rich-result eligibility in Google is
currently limited mainly to well-known authoritative health and government sites. Use FAQ
markup only where it accurately represents visible content and do not expect it to guarantee a
rich result. [4]
